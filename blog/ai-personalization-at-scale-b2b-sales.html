Relationships at Scale · Part 1

Every salesperson has a wish list. AI just made it possible.

Jim Sullivan · President · November 3, 2026 · Last updated November 3, 2026
Sales Execution

When I sit down with a client to talk about AI, I skip the technology at first. I ask two questions. What part of your job do you hate most? And if you had unlimited time, what would you do with it?

Ask a room of salespeople and the first answer barely varies: cold prospecting. The second answer is where it gets interesting. Their answers to that one describe, almost word for word, what personalization at scale looks like when AI does the heavy lifting in B2B sales.

This is Part 1 of Relationships at Scale, a series on how AI is finally making personalization at scale real in B2B sales. The Endurance Group (TEG) is a Claude Implementation Partner and the outsourced Claude team for B2B companies. We build the research, outreach, and follow-up systems described here.

What is personalization at scale in B2B sales?

It means giving every prospect the research and preparation a great salesperson once saved for their top ten accounts: the right person, a real reason to talk now, a genuine connection to open the door, and the right channel. AI now does the thinking behind that work, not just the writing, so a team can do it for hundreds of prospects while the salesperson keeps the relationship.

Key takeaways
  • The problem: real personalization takes research, timing and judgment, so a good rep can only do it for about a dozen accounts.
  • What changed: AI now does the thinking behind the outreach, not just the writing.
  • The proof: warm-path campaigns to shared-background alumni have produced 30% response rates, against low single digits for cold email.
  • The guardrail: use what helps the prospect, not everything you can find.

What would great salespeople do with unlimited time?

I have been in business development for more than 25 years, and I have asked that second question hundreds of times. The answers are remarkably consistent. With unlimited time, a great salesperson would:

  • Research the market to find the right companies, not just the ones that fit a filter in a database.
  • Find the real decision-maker inside each one, and the people who influence that decision.
  • Figure out why now: a new executive, a funding round, an expansion, a hiring push, a regulatory change, a competitor's move. Something that makes this person likely to be receptive this quarter rather than next year.
  • Look for the hidden connection. A shared alma mater, a former employer in common, a colleague at their own firm who already knows this person. This is social capital, and most organizations have far more of it than they realize. (We cover how to find warm paths into B2B prospects with Claude separately.)
  • Learn what matters to them as a person. The causes they champion, what they write and talk about publicly, the team they root for.
  • Choose the right channel. Some people answer email. Some only respond to a warm introduction. Some you will only ever meet at a trade show.
  • Build a personal strategy for each prospect, then actually carry it out without letting anyone fall through the cracks.

Then they keep going. Get the meeting on the calendar. Walk in prepared. Build trust, and know when the timing is right to ask for the business. Write the follow-up that moves things forward. Put together a proposal that speaks to what this buyer cares about. Spot the objection before it becomes the reason you lost.

Every sales leader I know agrees this is how selling should work. Almost nobody does it, for one simple reason: it does not scale. A good rep can do this level of work for maybe a dozen accounts. The rest get a template.

Why was personalization at scale always a broken promise?

"Personalization at scale" has been a marketing slogan for at least 15 years. What it usually meant in practice was mail merge: first name, company name, maybe a line about their industry. Buyers learned to spot it within a sentence.

The first wave of AI sales tools made it worse before it made it better. Suddenly every rep could produce a thousand emails that opened with "I saw you went to UCLA" or "Congrats on the new role." That is volume dressed up as personalization, and decision-makers are tuning it out faster than ever.

The issue was never that we lacked a way to write more personal sentences. The issue was that nobody had time to do the thinking: the research, the timing, the connection, the judgment about what is relevant to this one person.

What changed with AI?

What is different now is that AI can do the thinking, not just the writing. It can read a company's news, filings, job postings, and leadership changes. It can match a prospect's background against your team's networks to find who knows whom, judge why this person might care right now, and draft a message that sounds like you.

I learned the value of those connections early. I spent part of my career at Arthur Andersen, and so many people began their careers there that its alumni ended up leading finance teams, consulting practices, and C-suites across nearly every industry. "We both started at Andersen" earns more trust in five minutes than any cold call. Our outreach campaigns to fellow Andersen alumni have produced response rates of 30%, compared with the low single digits typical of cold email. That is why outsourced sales execution built on warm paths works.

LinkedIn helped me find those people, but it was only part of the map. It showed my connections, not my team's, and not my company's. Every colleague brings their own schools, former employers, and relationships, and those warm paths stayed invisible.

Closing that gap is why we built Commonality, and it deserves its own post later in this series. The broader lesson holds on its own: pair a real connection with a real reason to reach out, like the hiring signals we use to find companies ready to buy, and response rates look nothing like traditional outbound.

The "unlimited time" list is no longer a wish list. Every step on it can now be done for hundreds of prospects with the care a great rep once saved for their top ten. And none of it slows you down. Time still kills deals. The difference is that the homework that once took a week now happens before the first call.

Relationships have always created opportunity. AI does not change that. It gives salespeople the time to build more of them, and to build them well.

How do you keep AI personalization relevant, not creepy?

Here is the risk nobody selling AI sales tools likes to talk about. The same capability that lets you know a prospect well also lets you know too much. Mention someone's daughter's soccer team in a cold email and you have not built rapport. You have made them uneasy, and you have probably lost the deal.

The rule we follow is simple. Use what helps the prospect, not everything you can find. Business relevance and timing come first. Shared connections are fair game because they are the same thing a mutual friend would mention. Personal interests only belong in the conversation when there is a natural, earned reason to bring them up, usually in person and usually later. If you would not say it across a conference table at a first meeting, it does not go in the email.

Done right, the prospect should never think "this person used AI." They should think "this person did their homework."

Frequently asked questions

What does personalization at scale actually mean in B2B sales?

It means giving every prospect the level of research and preparation you used to reserve for your top five accounts: the right person, the right reason to talk now, the right connection to open the door, and the right channel to reach them. AI does the research and preparation. The salesperson keeps the relationship.

Isn't AI personalization just putting someone's name and alma mater into a template?

That is the version buyers have learned to ignore. Real personalization starts with relevance and timing: why this person, why now, and who you both know. Personal details only belong in a message when there is a genuine, natural reason to mention them.

How do you keep AI-driven prospecting from feeling creepy?

Use what helps the prospect, not everything you can find. Lead with business relevance and real shared connections, stay away from family and private details, and make sure every message sounds like the person sending it. If you would be uncomfortable saying it across a table, it does not go in the email.

Does AI replace the salesperson?

No. AI takes over the research, preparation, and follow-up work most salespeople dislike. The salesperson spends more of their time doing what actually wins business: earning trust in conversations and knowing when to ask for the sale.
