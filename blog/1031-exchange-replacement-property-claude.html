The best 1031 replacement property isn't the highest yield: how Claude helps owners see what they'd miss under the 45-day clock

Jim Sullivan · Founder · October 31, 2026
Real Estate

TEG is an official member of the Anthropic Claude Partner Network. We help real estate owners and teams put Claude to work on the decisions that matter most. See our full Claude services.

An owner sells a suburban office building. Within a week, the broker brings three replacement properties: an industrial building at a 6.0% cap rate, a grocery-anchored retail center at 7.0%, and an apartment community at 5.6%.

The broker likes the retail center. It has the highest yield, and the 45-day clock is running.

In this case, choosing the retail center would be a mistake, and it is a common one.

The short answer: A 1031 exchange lets an owner defer tax on a sale by reinvesting in like-kind property, on strict deadlines. In a 1031 exchange, owners tend to compare replacement properties with each other. The better question is what each one does to the portfolio you will own afterward. The highest-yield candidate is often the one that brings back the problem you sold to get rid of. Claude, Anthropic’s AI model, can build that portfolio view for every candidate in hours, so you get the answer while there is still time to use it.

I learned this long before AI. Early in my career I worked in Arthur Andersen’s real estate valuation practice, and later I underwrote commercial mortgage loans for securitization. A lender never judges a property on its own. It looks at the borrower’s whole exposure. Owners in an exchange should do the same, but the deadline pushes them to compare deals rather than portfolios.

What has changed is how quickly that portfolio view can be built. In our example, the family gave Claude four things: a portfolio summary with tenant names and addresses masked, the three offering memorandums, the expected closing date, and the family’s goals. In one working session, Claude:

  • Put all three candidates into the same one-page format, including what each memorandum left out
  • Rebuilt the whole portfolio three times, once with each candidate in place of the office building
  • Calculated the exchange deadlines and flagged that the tax return would come due before day 180
  • Pulled public records on each property and its seller, with a source for every item
  • Drafted the questions to ask each broker before the identification deadline

An analyst would usually need a couple of weeks for that work. With Claude, the family spent its 45 days deciding instead of compiling.

Why can the highest-yield 1031 replacement property be the wrong choice?

Look at why the owner is selling. The office building had heavy lease rollover: almost a third of its rent was coming due within three years. The family wanted steadier income and fewer management headaches.

Now look at the same three candidates in terms of what each one does to the whole portfolio:

The retail center pays the most, but 35% of its rent expires within three years. The family would be paying a premium to buy back the same rollover risk it is selling, in a market it doesn’t know.

The industrial building solves the real problem but creates a new one: a large share of rent depends on one tenant. Whether that is acceptable depends on that tenant’s credit, which the offering memorandum doesn’t show.

You can’t see any of this by comparing cap rates. Claude caught it because it was asked to rebuild the portfolio, not to rank the deals. How you frame the question to the AI matters as much as the AI itself. (The figures in this example are illustrative.)

What are the 1031 exchange deadlines, and is yours really 180 days?

Possibly not. You must identify replacement property within 45 days and receive it within 180 days, or by your tax return due date including extensions, whichever comes first. IRS: Instructions for Form 8824

The second part catches people. Suppose a sale closes in mid-November. Day 180 falls in mid-May, but the owner’s tax return is due April 15. Unless the return is extended, the exchange has to close almost a month earlier than the owner planned.

Your Qualified Intermediary and CPA own these dates. The practical point is simpler: you have less time than you think, so start the portfolio analysis before you list the property, not after you close.

What three questions should you ask about every 1031 replacement property?

After years of underwriting, I’ve found that three questions do most of the work:

  1. What problem am I selling, and does this property solve it or bring it back? Write down why you’re selling before you look at a single candidate.
  2. Is this a risk I already carry, or a new one? More of a risk you understand is different from a risk you’ve never managed.
  3. What would have to be true for this to be a good decision in two years? If you can’t name it, you can’t check it. Most owners never look back at whether their thesis held.

The questions are simple. Answering them for three or four candidates, against your actual portfolio, inside 45 days, is the hard part.

What won’t the offering memorandum tell you?

An offering memorandum is a sales document. It tells you what the seller wants you to focus on. Some of the most useful information is public, but it is scattered across county records, loan reporting, permits, and court filings that few buyers check during an exchange.

In our example, two findings changed the conversation:

  • The retail center’s seller had a loan maturing in eight months. That may explain the 7.0% asking cap rate, and it changes how you negotiate.
  • The industrial tenant had operated from the same building for 19 years with no liens or significant litigation. That isn’t a substitute for financial statements, but it told the family exactly what to ask for before naming the property.

Neither finding appeared in the memorandum. Claude found both by searching county and court records and citing a source for each, in hours rather than weeks. Coverage varies by county and state, and Claude says when it can’t find something.

Where does Claude fit, and where doesn’t it?

Claude handles the work that the deadline usually squeezes out. It can put every candidate into the same format, rebuild the portfolio with each one, gather public records with a source on every item, and draft a short investment memo that you can check in two years.

It does that consistently and quickly, and it works on masked data, so tenant names and addresses don’t need to leave your environment. The analysis is only as good as the data behind it: a wrong rent roll produces a wrong answer. Claude labels every figure as verified or estimated and lists what’s missing before it draws a conclusion.

Claude doesn’t decide which property you buy, and it doesn’t give tax advice. You make the decision, and your CPA and Qualified Intermediary handle the exchange. Our role is to make sure the AI does the analysis the way an experienced underwriter would.

Where doesn’t AI belong in a 1031 exchange?

AI doesn’t belong in the tax decision. As one Qualified Intermediary points out, an AI tool can’t see your true basis, your prior-year losses or carryforwards, or the rest of your tax history. Without those, it can’t tell you whether to do an exchange or how much gain you would defer. We agree.

So we draw a clear line. Whether to exchange, how much to defer, and every question about basis, boot, and compliance belong to your CPA and Qualified Intermediary. Which replacement property to buy is a portfolio decision, and that is where Claude earns its place. Keeping the two decisions separate is what makes the analysis trustworthy.

That doesn’t mean AI has no role on the tax side. Given complete records, such as prior returns, depreciation schedules, and closing statements, Claude can do much of the preparation: rolling basis forward, estimating gain and depreciation recapture, and comparing a full exchange with a partial one. What matters is who owns the answer. That work belongs in your CPA’s hands, and your CPA reviews and signs it. Our companion article for accountants shows how firms can put it to work.

Frequently asked questions

Why isn't the highest-yield 1031 replacement property always the best choice?

Yield measures one property by itself. An exchange changes your whole portfolio. A high-yield candidate can bring back the same lease rollover, concentration, or capital needs that led you to sell.

What are the 1031 exchange deadlines?

You must identify replacement property within 45 days of the sale. You must close within 180 days or by your tax return due date, including extensions, whichever comes first. Confirm your dates with your Qualified Intermediary.

Can Claude help evaluate 1031 replacement properties?

Yes. Claude can standardize each candidate, show how each one changes your portfolio, gather public records, and draft an investment memo. Decisions stay with the owner, and tax questions stay with the CPA and Qualified Intermediary.

Should you use AI for a 1031 exchange?

Not for the tax decision. Whether to exchange, and how much gain to defer, depend on your basis and tax history, so those questions belong with your CPA. AI is well suited to the property decision: comparing replacement candidates against your portfolio before the identification deadline.

What should you ask about every 1031 replacement property?

Ask what problem you are selling and whether the property solves it or brings it back, whether the risk is one you already carry or a new one, and what would have to be true for this to be a good decision in two years.
