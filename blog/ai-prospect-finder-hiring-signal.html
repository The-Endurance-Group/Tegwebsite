How We Use Hiring Signals to Find Companies Ready to Buy AI

Conor Sullivan · Vice President · August 4, 2026
AI Automations

One of the harder things about selling AI automation is that most companies aren't ready. Not because they don't want it, but because the buy-in hasn't fully landed. Maybe leadership approved something in theory but the person who'd actually use it every day hasn't been brought in. Maybe the tech team is on board but nobody's signed off on a budget yet. Either way, you end up in conversations that drag on or quietly die.

So I've been thinking about what a clean buy-in signal actually looks like, something that tells you the decision has already been made, not just that it's on someone's list.

The best one I've found: a company actively hiring for an AI Engineer or Automation Manager.

If someone is posting that job, the buy-in already happened. Both sides of the table agreed they needed someone. The budget was approved. A job description was written, reviewed, and posted. That's not a company that might get to AI someday. That's a company that's already moving.

Why most prospecting misses this signal

Standard outbound prospecting starts from a company list filtered by industry, headcount, or revenue. Those filters tell you who fits your general profile. They tell you almost nothing about where a company actually is in their decision process.

Filtering by job postings is different. You're not just filtering by who they are. You're filtering by what they're doing right now. The hiring signal tells you the internal decision has already been made, which means you're not trying to manufacture urgency from scratch. You're showing up at the right moment.

Most people have this data available and just aren't using it that way.

How the filter actually works

I built a system that pulls active job postings for roles like "AI Engineer" and "Automation Manager," then runs each result through our ICP criteria: industry, company size, a few other things.

The filter that matters most here is dropping native tech companies. A software company or a company with a full engineering org doesn't need an outside AI partner. They have engineers. What I'm looking for is a company in a non-tech industry, or a business without a dedicated technical team, that has just decided they need AI. That's the gap.

What's left after filtering is a short list of real companies with a demonstrated, live pain point and no in-house team to solve it.

What happens once a company qualifies

The automation doesn't stop at the list. For each qualifying company, it finds the person the new hire would actually report to. If a company is hiring for an AI role, there's a manager or VP somewhere who signed off on that req. That's the right contact: the person who owns the problem, not someone who'll pass the message along.

From there, it drafts a short personalized outreach note based on the role they're hiring for, and logs everything into our CRM with a follow-up task. Nothing sits in a spreadsheet waiting for someone to action it. It goes straight into the pipeline with a next step already attached.
