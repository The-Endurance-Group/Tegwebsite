How to automate business introductions with Claude

Conor Sullivan · Vice President · November 24, 2026
Professional Services

Short answer: yes. Claude can read your calendar for the past two weeks, research each person you met, check your email for existing ties, and pick the one pair worth introducing, or none. We built this as a Claude skill called Weekly Intro Matchmaker, and it drafts the introduction in Gmail for you to send.

Key takeaways

  • The skill reviews everyone you met with in the last 14 days and recommends one pair to introduce, or no pair.
  • An email check comes first. Pairs who already know each other, or where one referred the other, are dropped before anything else.
  • A tracking sheet remembers who has been researched, so each weekly run only studies new people.
  • Claude drafts the introduction in Gmail. You always send it yourself.

Why do good introductions fall through?

Most people who run a business meet a dozen interesting people a week and make almost no introductions. The reason is rarely generosity. Making a good introduction means remembering who you met, understanding what each person wants and offers, confirming they have not already met, and writing a message that makes both sides glad you did it. That is an hour of work with no deadline, so it slips.

A good introduction is also one of the most useful things you can give a professional contact. It costs you nothing, and it earns trust on both sides. That makes it a strong candidate for a Claude skill, because the work is research and judgment that follows the same steps every week.

What is Weekly Intro Matchmaker?

Weekly Intro Matchmaker is a Claude skill that turns your recent meetings into one introduction recommendation. A Claude skill is a saved set of instructions Claude follows the same way every time you ask. This one works in six steps:

  1. Builds a roster from your calendar. It lists the external people you met with in the last 14 days and drops your own team and family.
  2. Loads what it already knows. A Google Sheet holds a profile for every person it has researched, plus every pair it has already considered.
  3. Researches only new or stale people. Each profile covers what the person wants, what they offer, and what must stay private.
  4. Shortlists pairs. It looks for similar backgrounds, shared interests, and a plausible business relationship where each side gains something.
  5. Checks Gmail. Any pair already connected is removed.
  6. Drafts one introduction. The draft is saved in Gmail, addressed to both people, with a specific detail about each.

Why does the email check come before everything else?

The email check exists because our first test went wrong. The first version of the workflow read only meeting summaries from a week of calls. It confidently recommended introducing two commercial real estate firms because both had the same problem with independent contractors using AI tools on their own.

It was a good pairing on paper. It was also pointless, because one of those firms had referred the other to us two weeks earlier. A search of Gmail showed the connection immediately. The meeting summaries never would have.

That mistake set the rule the skill follows now. Before any pair is shortlisted, Claude searches email for threads that link the two people or their companies, in both directions. If they share a thread, a referral, or a mutual introducer, the pair is dropped. The same lesson shaped the roster: it comes from the calendar rather than from meeting recordings, because the calendar includes people you met without recording the call. Recordings are used last, only to add detail once a pair is chosen.

How does Claude avoid researching the same people every week?

Claude keeps a tracking sheet with two tabs, one for people and one for pairs. The people tab stores a business summary, what each person wants, what they offer, background, a short meeting summary, and notes on what is safe to share. A person researched in the last 60 days is read from the sheet instead of researched again. The pairs tab records every combination already considered and what happened to it, so the same pair is never proposed twice.

When someone is new, the research escalates only as far as it needs to:

  1. Your saved notes, then the company website and a web search.
  2. The person's LinkedIn profile.
  3. Meeting recordings, if they exist.
  4. If that is still not enough, the person is skipped rather than guessed at.

The "safe to share" column matters more than it looks. A call may include details a contact would not want repeated to someone else. The skill checks that column before it puts any detail into an introduction.

What happens when no pair is a good match?

When no pair clears the bar, the skill reports no introduction. It names the closest pair, which we call the near miss, and explains why it fell short, for example no clear benefit to both sides, a mismatch in size or location, or too little known about one person. It drafts nothing.

We built that behavior on purpose. A weak introduction spends your credibility on both people at once. A skipped week costs nothing. Existing clients are not excluded, since connecting your own clients with each other is often the best introduction available. The only hard exclusion is people who already know each other.

How do you run it every week?

You can run it by asking Claude for your weekly intro check, or put it on a schedule. We run ours as a weekly scheduled task so the draft is waiting in Gmail at the start of the day. The guide to scheduling a task in Claude walks through the setup, and Anthropic documents how scheduled tasks work in its help center. The skill itself follows Anthropic's published Agent Skills format, and it reaches your calendar and email through Claude connectors.

What should you know before building one?

The skill is only as good as the data it can see. A calendar full of unlabeled meetings gives it little to work with, and an email history that lives in several inboxes will miss ties. It also never sends anything. Every introduction is a draft that you read and change, and that review step is what keeps the voice yours.

The same pattern applies beyond introductions. Any recurring judgment call that starts with "who should I..." can be built this way: a roster, a memory of what has been done, a hard filter that rules out bad answers, and a draft for you to approve. Frequently asked questions

Can Claude decide which two of my contacts should meet?

Yes. With access to your calendar, email, and the web, Claude can compare the people you met with recently, rule out pairs who already know each other, and pick the one pair with a real reason to talk. If no pair clears the bar, it says so.

Does Weekly Intro Matchmaker send the introduction for me?

No. It saves the introduction as a Gmail draft addressed to both people. You read it, edit it, and send it yourself.

What does Claude need access to for this?

Google Calendar to list who you met with, Gmail to check for existing ties and save the draft, and a Google Sheet to remember who it has already researched. Meeting recordings are optional and only used to add detail once a pair is chosen.

What if none of the people I met with are a good fit for each other?

Then the right answer is no introduction. The skill names the closest pair, explains why it fell short, and drafts nothing. A forced match costs you more trust than a skipped week.
